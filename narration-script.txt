c01s03 Chapter 1: A behaviour is more specific than an ambition.
Behavioural science brings psychology into conversation with economics, sociology and other fields. A target behaviour names an action and its circumstances. An outcome states why that action matters.

For the café, completing a booking is an action; receiving a useful repair is a wider outcome. More clicks could leave that outcome unchanged if no appointments are available.

c01s03b Chapter 1: A missed action can have several causes.
COM-B organizes questions about capability, opportunity and motivation. It is a diagnostic framework, not a test that reveals a cause by itself.

An unclear instruction may create a capability barrier. A printer requirement or unsuitable opening time may constrain opportunity. Competing priorities can affect motivation. These explanations can coexist and need evidence.

c01s04 Chapter 1: Understanding a barrier needs more than a label.
Behavioural diagnosis uses information about the actual process. Qualitative evidence can reveal barriers that a completion count hides. Triangulation compares those accounts with other evidence.

Ask people about the booking route, inspect the form, and compare where attempts end. A survey saying motivation is low does not establish that an encouraging message will improve access.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
The behaviour change wheel describes COM-B as a framework for intervention design. Its categories help organize an assessment; placing a problem in a category does not establish the cause or the effectiveness of a proposed solution.

A course develops interpretation and design skills. It cannot remove a service’s staffing, transport or funding constraints.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Count the work from the participant’s side.
Administrative burden concerns costs experienced in an interaction. Learning cost includes finding a rule; compliance cost includes completing the required steps.

An instruction that looks brief to its author may require travel, documents and repeated calls. Map the journey from the participant’s position, including failed attempts. The number of screens alone does not measure the total burden.

c02s03b Chapter 2: The same process can impose unequal costs.
Psychological cost includes experiences such as stigma or an intrusive demand. Accessibility concerns whether different people can use the route. A distributional effect describes who carries the costs or receives the benefit.

Jo’s printer constraint differs from someone else’s language or mobility needs. Ask about relevant conditions without assuming that everyone in a demographic group has the same experience.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Simplifying a form does not create appointments.
Friction can come from unnecessary steps. A structural constraint, such as limited evening transport, can require a different response. Service capacity limits how many repairs can actually be offered.

Removing duplicate questions may help Jo complete the form. It does not add an evening appointment or a bus route. Compare proposed changes with the barrier they are meant to address.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Administrative-burden research distinguishes learning, compliance and psychological costs. These categories help investigate unequal access, but they do not imply that every requirement is unnecessary. Some requirements serve legitimate safety or eligibility purposes.

Evaluate simplification alongside its consequences, service capacity and accessible alternatives. A shorter process is not sufficient evidence of a fairer outcome.

c03s03 Chapter 3: A message competes with other demands.
Limited attention means that relevant information may not come to mind when needed. Salience concerns what stands out; a reminder is a cue intended to bring an action back to mind.

A clear appointment time near the moment of planning may help. A brightly coloured message can also be noticed without making the visit feasible. Distinguish noticing from understanding and acting.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Use the channel and moment that fit the action.
Timing matters because a cue can arrive before a person can act. Actionable information specifies the next step, including how to change a booking. Notification fatigue is a possible cost of repeated or unwanted messages.

A visitor may prefer one useful reminder with a rescheduling link over daily messages. Check preferences and access to the channel rather than assuming that more contact is better.

c03s04 Chapter 3: An opened message is not an attended appointment.
A process measure, such as message delivery, can help explain implementation. A behavioural outcome records the action that matters. A mechanism hypothesis connects the two but still needs evidence.

If openings rise while attendance is unchanged, the message may attract attention without resolving access. Also check whether the attendance measure is reliable and whether the service had room for more visitors.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Field experiments have found useful reminder effects in particular savings settings. Those findings do not establish that any reminder, channel or frequency will work for café attendance or another service.

Test the proposed timing and content against a suitable comparison, using a meaningful outcome and respecting communication preferences.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Equivalent descriptions can guide attention differently.
A framing effect concerns the description of a choice. A reference point supplies a comparison standard. Loss aversion describes a pattern in some models and settings; it is not a universal rule about every person.

Calling a membership change a gain or a loss can highlight different comparisons. Changing the actual price or eligibility is a different intervention from changing the description.

c04s03b Chapter 4: A default can have more than one explanation.
A default option applies when someone does not actively select otherwise. Status quo bias can contribute to staying with an existing state, but inattention, switching effort and perceived endorsement can also matter. Active choice asks for an explicit decision.

An automatic renewal could increase retention while leaving some people with an unwanted charge. Its uptake does not show that every retained member preferred it.

c04s04 Chapter 4: A higher uptake rate is not the whole evaluation.
Informed choice needs understandable information. A switching cost can make an unwanted option hard to escape. Welfare concerns the consequences for people, not simply how many memberships the service retains.

Show the price and renewal date clearly, offer an accessible cancellation route, and ask about unwanted charges. A default should not be treated as permission to hide information or manufacture agreement.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Research documents framing and default effects in specified settings, including retirement-saving decisions. Multiple mechanisms and contextual differences limit simple predictions about a new service.

A model of loss aversion is not a licence to invent threats or obscure a price. Ethical evaluation includes understanding, accessible alternatives and unwanted consequences alongside uptake.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Delay can reflect more than one process.
Present bias concerns disproportionate emphasis on immediacy; time inconsistency concerns a change in preferred plans as a decision approaches. A constraint can instead make action genuinely unavailable.

Sam might value volunteering but avoid today’s form. Jo may be unable to select a shift without a work schedule. Observing delay alone cannot identify either person’s preferences or prove a self-control problem.

c05s03b Chapter 5: A plan needs a usable cue and a feasible response.
An implementation intention links a cue to a response: if a situation occurs, then take a specified action. Feasibility still matters.

Sam might choose to book after receiving the next work schedule. That cue makes sense only if a suitable shift is available and the booking route works. An if-then sentence cannot supply transport, money or predictable working hours.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Commitment can support a goal or impose a cost.
A commitment device changes the conditions around a future choice. Voluntary participation and reversibility matter because circumstances can change.

Sam may want a calendar booking and an easy way to reschedule. A large cancellation penalty could instead punish an unavoidable shift change. Evaluate the aid with the person’s interests in view, including who bears the risk of an unexpected problem.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Present-biased models explain some patterns of changing choice; they do not diagnose a person from a missed task. Research on implementation intentions examines specified plans, tasks and populations, with effects that depend on those conditions.

A planning aid needs an existing goal, a feasible response and an acceptable burden. It cannot substitute for material resources or predictable access.

c06s03 Chapter 6: What people do and what they approve are different.
A descriptive norm concerns what people commonly do. An injunctive norm concerns approval. A reference group specifies whose behaviour or approval is relevant.

A sign about other café visitors differs from a statement about national habits. Neither establishes what this visitor values. Before using a norm message, check that its claim is accurate and its comparison group makes sense.

c06s03b Chapter 6: An average can point people in an unwanted direction.
A boomerang effect moves behaviour opposite to the intended direction. A heterogeneous effect varies across people or settings. Baseline behaviour can help identify which comparisons might matter.

A careful tool borrower who sees that the average return rate is lower than their own could infer that doing less is normal. That is a hypothesis to consider, not a prediction that every careful borrower will change.

c06s04 Chapter 6: A truthful sign still needs an accessible action.
Social context affects which comparisons are meaningful. Mechanism evidence helps distinguish social influence from other explanations. An unintended consequence can include exclusion or an unwanted change among people already doing well.

A sign might work because it clarifies where tools belong rather than because visitors compare themselves with others. Check the shelf design and evaluate the action, not just agreement with the message.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
An energy-use field experiment found that descriptive comparisons could have different effects depending on prior consumption, and that an approval cue changed those results in that setting. This supports attention to baselines and message content, not a universal recipe for every group.

Do not invent a majority claim to obtain compliance. A truthful, relevant message can still require testing and monitoring for uneven or unwanted effects.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Ask exactly what the arrangement rewards.
An incentive changes the relative attractiveness of actions. Reimbursement repays a cost; opportunity cost concerns the alternative a person gives up. These can overlap in practice but answer different questions.

Travel reimbursement may make a shift affordable. A payment for repair count rewards a particular result. Neither proposal should be evaluated by a general claim that money either solves motivation or damages it.

c07s03b Chapter 7: The rewarded measure can displace the intended outcome.
A proxy measure stands in for something harder to observe. A substitution effect can shift activity toward the rewarded action. Gaming improves the measured result without the intended benefit.

Counting completed repairs may encourage quick cases, or change how a completion is recorded. Measure quality, difficult-case access and unintended costs alongside throughput. Not every rise in the count is gaming, so investigate rather than presume misconduct.

c07s04 Chapter 7: Meaning and fairness can affect a response.
Motivational crowding describes a possible change in other motivation. Acceptability concerns how people judge an arrangement, while distribution concerns who gains and who pays its costs.

A payment can communicate recognition, control or a commercial expectation depending on the setting. Ask affected people, compare meaningful outcomes, and examine who can meet the rewarded target. Do not turn a possible crowding effect into a rule against paying people fairly.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Research reviews describe varied incentive effects across tasks, populations and arrangements. Effects on other motivation are possible, but they do not justify the blanket claim that payments backfire or that unpaid participation is preferable.

Evaluate the specified arrangement over an appropriate period. Preserve fair compensation and examine quality, access, costs and effects beyond the rewarded measure.

c08s03 Chapter 8: A theory of change makes the links inspectable.
A theory of change links a change to a proposed process and outcome. An assumption states a condition that link relies on. A counterfactual asks what would happen under an alternative condition.

A reminder assumes that forgetting is relevant, the message is understood, and a feasible appointment exists. Compare it with the current approach and with a material response such as suitable opening hours.

c08s03b Chapter 8: Design tools have different purposes.
Choice architecture concerns how options are arranged. A nudge changes that environment while preserving options and avoiding a substantial change in economic incentives. A boost aims to develop decision-making competence. These are useful distinctions with debated boundaries.

A clear comparison display may help a choice now; teaching a reusable comparison skill has a different purpose. Neither replaces a needed repair slot, and neither label guarantees effectiveness.

c08s04 Chapter 8: A memorable checklist is not evidence of impact.
A design framework can help generate ideas. A pilot or feasibility study addresses specified practical questions; a small positive result does not automatically establish effectiveness.

Check whether staff can deliver the change, visitors can use it, and outcomes can be measured. Then use an appropriate evaluation. Keep a record of rejected alternatives so a convenient message does not crowd out a better response to the diagnosed barrier.

Publication bias means that the published record can depend on which results are obtained. Reports of attractive successes may omit null or unwanted results. Seek a fuller record when assessing broad claims.

c08s05 Chapter 8: What does the evidence allow?
Choice-architecture reviews reach different conclusions depending on included studies and assumptions about publication bias. Large operational trials also show that published academic effects should not be transferred mechanically to a new service.

A design framework or the word nudge does not establish a benefit. State the mechanism, alternatives, uncertainty and ethical conditions of the specific proposal.

c09s03 Chapter 9: Before-and-after is not the only possible comparison.
A causal effect compares an intervention with a specified alternative. Random assignment uses chance to allocate an assignment unit, such as a person or a workshop.

A simultaneous comparison can help distinguish the reminder from changes in weather or available slots. If people share messages within a workshop, consider whether assigning workshops is more appropriate. Random assignment allocates units to conditions; random sampling selects units from a population using chance. These procedures answer different questions.

c09s03b Chapter 9: Keep the denominator that matches the question.
Intention-to-treat analysis compares people in their assigned conditions, including those who did not open a message. A percentage point is a difference between percentages. Attrition concerns missing outcome observations.

In an illustrative comparison, attendance of sixty percent versus fifty percent is ten percentage points higher. Comparing only people who opened the message can select different people and break the original comparison.

c09s04 Chapter 9: A point estimate is not the whole result.
A confidence interval describes uncertainty under statistical assumptions. A wide interval may leave both little benefit and a substantial average benefit compatible with the data. It is not a guarantee about a specific individual. Preregistration records important decisions before results are inspected. Interference occurs when one unit’s treatment affects another’s outcome.

Specify the outcome, comparison, timing, missing-data approach and analysis before testing many versions. Check implementation, spillovers and uncertainty. A favourable estimate alone does not establish a reliable or transferable benefit.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?
Randomized evaluations can support causal inference when assignment, measurement and analysis are appropriate. They do not automatically solve interference, missing outcomes, selective reporting or limited applicability to other settings.

These fictional exercises teach interpretation. Real field research needs suitable expertise, permissions, participant protections and governance; an educational course does not provide that authorization.

c10s03 Chapter 10: An average can hide different consequences.
An average treatment effect summarizes a defined comparison, not a benefit for every person. Equity asks about fair opportunities and distribution. Opportunity cost concerns what another use of the resources could achieve.

An attendance gain could coexist with unwanted contacts or exclusion of people needing a phone route. Examine these consequences and compare feasible alternatives, including a service-capacity change. A dashboard average cannot make the value judgment on its own.

c10s03b Chapter 10: Protect choice throughout the process.
Autonomy concerns meaningful control over choices. Transparency makes relevant purposes and conditions understandable. Data minimization limits collection to a justified need.

Use an accessible way to stop reminders and avoid collecting detailed personal information just because a system permits it. Where consent or review is required, obtain it before implementation. A benign goal does not excuse misleading claims or an obstructive exit.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Scaling is another claim to investigate.
External validity concerns applicability beyond the studied conditions. Implementation fidelity concerns how a change is delivered. Monitoring tracks specified benefits and harms over time.

A new site may differ in staffing, language, trust or available services. Plan adaptation and a suitable further evaluation, with criteria for pausing or stopping. Keep null and unwanted results visible so later decisions are not based only on success stories.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
Large operational trials show that effects and their interpretation depend on setting, implementation and the evidence available for comparison. Debate about choice-architecture meta-analysis also highlights how publication and analytic assumptions can affect broad claims.

Neither a positive average nor a behavioural label settles welfare, fairness or consent. State the decision criteria, relevant uncertainty, alternatives and conditions for further review.
