# Learning design

Audience: interested adult beginners who want to interpret human behaviour using evidence. The gap is between naming a plausible influence on behaviour and developing a feasible, testable and ethical change proposal. Training addresses interpretation and design skills; it does not fix service capacity or material constraints. Learners choose the course voluntarily and can begin here using the recaps. Delivery is a standalone, accessible, learner-paced GitHub Pages course, not an LMS or certification.

The established café visual identity uses Libre Baskerville headings, Arimo body text, pale blue backgrounds, navy text, teal interactions and restrained chapter accents. Reading and controls precede the supporting figure in the stacked layout. The primary learning path remains clear when research disclosures stay closed.

Each chapter has a first decision, three separate narrated explanations, a visible evidence check, a new written transfer case, unaided retrieval and low-stakes field practice. The final case integrates chapters. Page visits indicate navigation, not mastery. No learner pilot or independent academic review has been conducted.

# Needs analysis and assessment standard

Success means a learner can specify an action and meaningful outcome, compare at least two plausible barriers or responses, identify a defensible comparison, and address uncertainty, access and participant interests. Formative decisions expose common design errors; written transfer and the five-part final case require reasoning in a new context. Visits and correct recognition are not evidence of general mastery. Independent review and learner testing remain outstanding.

# Visual plan and critique

The established series identity is intentional: navy #19394D text, pale blue #ECF4F7 background, teal #216B88 interactions, purple #7058A3 comparisons, gold #D8AC5C accents and white reading surfaces. Libre Baskerville supports titles; Arimo supports readable body text. Quiet controls separate the text from the supporting figure on narrow screens. Each figure has one teaching purpose: a sequence only when a process is being taught, a comparison only when two interpretations need separating. Forty figures occupy their own teaching pages; research remains optional. The initial idea of making every proposal a nudge was rejected because it would hide material alternatives and disputed evidence.

## 1. Define the action before choosing a fix

Objective: Given a change proposal, specify an observable behaviour and distinguish capability, opportunity and motivation as competing explanations.

Initial decision: Which is the most useful next step in this café case?

Transfer: A community service wants to improve access for residents working daytime shifts. Which target is sufficiently specific to investigate?

Field application: Choose a fictional service problem. Write a target behaviour, a meaningful outcome, and one capability, opportunity and motivation hypothesis. Do not label real people from a distance.

## 2. Friction is not always a small inconvenience

Objective: Given a service journey, identify learning, compliance and psychological costs, and distinguish simplification from changes to material access.

Initial decision: What should the team compare first?

Transfer: A redesigned form has higher completion, but evening appointments remain unavailable and some visitors still need a phone route. What conclusion is supported?

Field application: Map a fictional booking journey. Identify one requirement’s purpose, its likely costs, an accessible alternative, and a material constraint that a form redesign would leave unchanged.

## 3. A reminder needs something feasible to remind

Objective: Given a proposed reminder, distinguish an attention hypothesis from lack of access, and specify a meaningful test outcome.

Initial decision: Which proposal best tests an attention explanation?

Transfer: A new reminder gets more openings, but the attendance rate is unchanged. What should the team investigate next?

Field application: Draft a fictional appointment reminder. Name the attention hypothesis, the feasible next step, an accessible alternative, and the outcome you would compare with the current approach.

## 4. The way a choice is presented can matter

Objective: Given a choice interface, identify a frame or default, distinguish presentation from a changed option, and protect informed choice.

Initial decision: What should the team establish before adopting automatic renewal?

Transfer: A service wants to test whether gain-oriented versus loss-oriented wording affects a voluntary contribution. What comparison isolates presentation most clearly?

Field application: On paper, design an honest choice between two fictional service options. Identify the reference point, any default, information needed for understanding, and a low-effort way to change a choice.

## 5. A future intention meets a present situation

Objective: Given a missed intention, distinguish present bias from constraints and design a feasible, voluntary planning aid.

Initial decision: What best distinguishes Sam’s and Jo’s possible barriers?

Transfer: A visitor wants to volunteer but can choose a shift only after receiving a variable work schedule. Which planning aid best fits the case?

Field application: Write an optional if-then plan for a fictional intention. State the cue, the response, a constraint that could defeat it, and a fair way to revise the arrangement.

## 6. A social norm message needs a truthful comparison

Objective: Given a norm message, distinguish descriptive and injunctive claims, identify the relevant comparison group, and assess a possible boomerang effect.

Initial decision: What should the team do before using Leo’s proposed sign?

Transfer: In a fictional pilot, average returns rise slightly but returns among previously careful borrowers fall. The groups are small. What does the combined result suggest?

Field application: Draft a norm message only for a fictional dataset. State the observation behind it, the reference group, a possible unintended response, and a practical alternative to a message.

## 7. An incentive can change more than the payoff

Objective: Given an incentive proposal, identify the rewarded measure, possible substitution and distributional costs, and specify outcomes beyond the rewarded count.

Initial decision: Which evaluation best matches the café proposal?

Transfer: A pilot reward increases completed-repair counts, but difficult repairs are now referred elsewhere more often. What is a proportionate interpretation?

Field application: Design a fictional incentive evaluation. Identify the rewarded action, a meaningful outcome, one possible substitution, and a fairness or access measure.

## 8. Build a proposal with a mechanism and alternatives

Objective: Given a diagnosed barrier, construct a theory of change, compare behavioural and material responses, and explain what evidence would challenge the proposal.

Initial decision: How should the team select a proposal?

Transfer: Staff and visitors try a prototype with no comparison group. They identify unclear instructions and a delivery problem. What can this pilot most directly support?

Field application: On paper, compare two responses to a fictional barrier, including one material or service change. Write a theory of change for each and one observation that would challenge it.

## 9. Compare changes with what would have happened

Objective: Given a fictional evaluation, identify the comparison and assignment unit, interpret an intention-to-treat difference, and state important sources of uncertainty.

Initial decision: What is the central problem with Leo’s claim?

Transfer: A fictional trial randomly assigns eligible visitors to two reminder approaches. Some messages are not opened and some attendance records are missing. Which comparison best preserves the intended randomized question?

Field application: Draft an evaluation plan for a fictional service change. Specify the comparator, assignment unit, meaningful outcome, denominator, missing-data concern and a possible spillover. Do not conduct a real experiment from this exercise.

## 10. A useful result still needs an ethical decision

Objective: Given a positive evaluation, judge whether to adapt, expand, investigate or stop using evidence on benefit, distribution, autonomy, implementation and uncertainty.

Initial decision: What should the café do before expanding the pilot?

Transfer: A pilot improved average attendance, but a new site lacks an accessible alternative and some participants found the messages intrusive. Which recommendation is best supported?

Field application: Review your fictional proposal using benefit, equity, autonomy, privacy and uncertainty. Write one condition for adaptation, one for further evaluation, and one for stopping.
